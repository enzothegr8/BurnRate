### Where each one goes

- **Descriptor**: LinkedIn headline, meta description, anywhere a stranger needs to know what this is in one second. Boring on purpose.
- **The line**: homepage, About page, top of a pitch. This is the thesis. Never shorten it by replacing the period with a colon; the period is what gives the second half its landing.
- **Short form**: email signature, 160-character bios, the back of a card, under the wordmark where the full line won't fit.

### Why the name works, so it doesn't get diluted

A burn is an engine firing. Burn rate is the most important number in startup finance. Same phrase, both industries, no pun and no wink. Every design and editorial decision below is downstream of that one property: **one object, two meanings, no cleverness on display.**

## 2. Positioning

**What Burn Rate is:** the place where the money side of the space industry is counted, sourced, and explained. Economics, capital, contracts, and the politics and regulation that move capital.

**What Burn Rate is not:**

- Not a space news site. Launches, science, and hardware appear only where they move money.
- Not a fund, a syndicate, or an advisor. Never use "Capital," "Ventures," "Partners," or "Advisors" in any name, product, or URL. Readers must understand that Burn Rate analyzes money and does not deploy it.
- Not a wonder publication. See §7.

**The competitive set:** Payload, Space Capital, Seraphim, Novaspace, BryceTech, SpaceNews. They have teams, funds, and paid data. Burn Rate does not compete on breadth. It competes on **owning one dataset completely and being visibly more honest than anyone else about what it knows.**

**The authority problem, stated plainly.** Enzo is 20, has no flight hardware, and no institution behind him. Authority cannot be borrowed. It is substituted with radical transparency: showing uncertainty on the surface of the page where nobody can miss it. This is a real advantage precisely because it isn't available to the incumbents. They don't need it, and it would only cost them.

## 3. Voice

**The register is cold and clear, not awed and not snide.**

Rules:

- **Never open with awe.** No "humanity's ultimate frontier," no "final frontier," no Star Trek register anywhere in body copy or headlines. The grandeur is confined to the positioning line, where a reader has already opted in.
- **Never adopt the dunking tone.** Contempt for the discourse is earned by people who ship hardware. Rigor is the personality; attitude is not.
- **Flag your own uncertainty before anyone asks.** The Caveats habit is the voice. "ILRS budget is genuinely opaque, do not invent one" is the house style in one sentence.
- **Do the division.** The interesting number is almost never the published one. $132B of $613B is data. "78 cents of every dollar in space is private money now" is a read. The read is what people repeat and what gets you hired.
- **American English. No em dashes.** Use commas, periods, or parentheses.
- **Date everything.** "Figures as of July 15, 2026" appears on every artifact. It costs nothing and it is a credibility signal.

**Reference sentences** (the voice at its best, from existing work):

> A budget is a promise, and promises are not receipts.

> The money so far grades the road. The building has not been ordered.

> Until real money flows to habitats and power, the "base" is a scouting mission with excellent branding.

## 4. The notation

**This is the brand.** More than the palette, more than the type, more than any logo. It makes editorial values legible at a glance: a reader learns in two seconds, without being told, that this publication distinguishes between what it knows, what it's been told, and what it's guessing.

| Mark | Means | Color |
|---|---|---|
| Solid underline | **Confirmed.** Primary source, verifiable, unambiguous. | Ink `#16181B` |
| Dashed underline | **Reported.** Credible source, but unverified or inconsistently stated across sources. | Muted `#8C9198` |
| Dotted underline | **Derived or estimated.** Burn Rate's own calculation or judgment. | Ochre `#A8823C` |

Applied to the number itself, 1.5px, `padding-bottom: 1px`.

**Consequences that are not optional:**

- **Links can never be underlined.** The underline budget is spent. Links are secondary color `#565A5F`, no underline.
- **Orange is not in the notation.** Orange has one job and it is emphasis. See §5.
- **Every unmarked number is an implicit claim.** Once this system exists, an unmarked figure reads as an assertion. There is no neutral.
- **The system indicts you.** One dotted line where a solid should have been is a bigger failure than a plain publication ever risks. That is the point.

Full operational rules, including who may promote a number and how agents interact with it, live in `editorial-standards.md`.

| Role | Hex | Notes |

| Vellum (page) | `#EAEBE5` | The signature. Engineering drafting paper, not white. |
| Card | `#F5F6F2` | Raised surfaces, sparingly. |
| Rule | `#D3D6CE` | Hairlines and dividers. |
| Muted | `#8C9198` | Labels, metadata, the "reported" dash. |
| Secondary | `#565A5F` | Supporting copy, links. |
| Ink | `#16181B` | Body text, confirmed underline, hollow bar outlines. |
| Orange | `#E8410A` | International Orange. Emphasis only. |
| Ochre | `#A8823C` | The "derived" dotted line only. |

### Series (charts, only when there are genuinely categories)

`#E8410A` · `#37535F` · `#A8823C` · `#6E7F63` · neutral `#8C9198`

### Rules

- **Light only.** No dark mode. Dark codes as crypto, terminal, and gaming, and it is miserable for long reads. Analyst-grade publications are light.
- **The vellum is not negotiable.** It is the single most recognizable property of the site, the way FT is pink. Never ship on white.
- **Cream is forbidden.** `#F4EFE1` and its neighbors belong to The Space Race. So do `#E9B84C` gold and `#E5544C` red. Never use them.
- **Orange appears once per section, and it always means "look here."** If two things are orange, neither is. Count it on every page.
- **Charts use emphasis, not rainbow.** One hue plus grey. The series palette is for real categories, not for decoration.

## 6. Type

| Role | Face | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Display | **Instrument Serif** | Headlines and claim sentences. Free. |
| Body | **IBM Plex Sans** | Running copy and UI. Commissioned as engineering documentation type. |
| Data | **IBM Plex Mono** | Every number, label, timestamp, and source line. |

### Rules

- **Serif headlines are the differentiator.** Every space publication uses a geometric sans. A serif headline puts you in the room with the FT and out of the room with the blogs.
- **Every number is mono, with `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums`.** No exceptions. Figures that don't align in a column are the mark of an amateur publication, and this is a publication about numbers. Mono for figures is what makes a page look audited.
- **Not Inter.** It's the default of the entire internet and it's also The Space Race's body face.
- **Known constraint:** Instrument Serif ships regular and italic only, no bold. It is a display face, not a text family. Subheads and pull quotes take weight from IBM Plex Sans, or a second serif gets chosen later. Do not fake weight.

## 7. Design principles

These are the rules that make a page feel like Burn Rate rather than like a dashboard.

**1. No number without a claim.**
Every figure gets a sentence saying what it means. Labels are not language. A terminal has no voice; a publication does. This is the difference between "this person has data" and "this person has judgment," and judgment is the thing worth hiring.

**2. The atomic unit is a gap, not a value.**
A value just sits there. The beat is the distance between what was announced and what was signed. Every module should be built from two numbers and the space between them. Tension is intrigue; a value is furniture.

**3. Promises are hollow. Receipts are solid.**
A pledged figure is drawn as an outline. A signed figure is drawn as a fill. This is a law across every chart on the site. It renders the tagline literally and nobody else does it.

**4. No tiles. Scale contrast instead.**
Equal boxes in a grid make a page inert. Editorial pages breathe through variation: one thing enormous, one thing wide, one thing small.

**5. Use the interesting number, not the available one.**
$613B is in every space deck on earth. "78 cents of every dollar" is yours, because you did the division. Same data, different publication.

**6. Hairlines, not cards.**
Rules and whitespace divide. No drop shadows, no floating rounded boxes, no gradients. Technical documents don't have shadows.

**7. Density is not the goal.**
"Wow, the whole industry at my fingertips" is unwinnable; Bloomberg already exists. The wow that matters is "wow, this person noticed something I didn't." Fewer things, said properly.

## 8. Image doctrine

**The rule is not "no images." The rule is: every image must be evidence, not atmosphere.**

The test is one question: *if you delete it, does the reader lose information?* A photo of the wrecked LC-36 pad is evidence. A nebula behind a headline is wallpaper.

- **No starfields, no nebulas, no artist's renders of lunar habitats.** Every space outlet leads with awe imagery. The visual absence of awe is the positioning.
- **Photographs are never bare.** They arrive annotated, in IBM Plex Mono, with leader lines pointing at the thing that matters. This makes decoration structurally impossible, because the annotation forces you to state why the image is there. The annotation layer is what makes a public-domain photo *yours*.
- **Hardware is drawn, not rendered.** Orthographic line drawings, the way NASA program documentation has always done it. Consistent, cheap to maintain, and it looks nothing like the render-heavy competition.
- **Icons are drafted, not decorative.** One stroke weight, one system.
- **NASA imagery is public domain.** Nearly every photo of hardware, pads, and landers is free to use with attribution. The image problem is largely already solved.

## 9. Components

The recurring objects. Build each once, use everywhere.

**The counter.** The hero. An annual figure divided into seconds, ticking. Currently the $613B global space economy at ~$19,438/sec. It is a *rate visualization*, not a measurement, and that must be visible: the derived rate carries the dotted line, the source figure carries the solid line, the vintage is stated. Labeled, it is honest. Unlabeled, it is a lie and it destroys the notation. If it ever reads as a stunt, shrink it; do not remove it.

**The gap module.** Kicker, claim sentence in Instrument Serif, hollow bar (promise) above solid bar (receipt) on a shared scale, then a paragraph of voice, then a link deeper. This is the page's workhorse.

**The ledger row.** Recipient, descriptor, date, value with notation. Hairline separated. No card.

**The title block.** Engineering drawing convention at the foot of anything with numbers in it: DOC · REV · UPDATED · DRAWN · SOURCES. The revision number is orange, because the revision is the thing that changed. Scope it to trackers, models, and deep dives; it is precious on a 400-word news post.

**Revisions.** Articles are living documents, not archives. The beat moves fast enough that pieces go stale in weeks, so they carry Rev numbers and update dates. This solves a real editorial problem and no competitor does it.

## 10. The logo

**Deliberately open.** Explored and rejected so far: the segmented tank, the fuel gauge, the disc, the delta, the dial, the ring, the nozzle bell, the flame, the decaying plume, the chevron, the de Laval throat, the candlestick, the orbit arc, the burn-down curve, the receipt, the Hohmann transfer.

**What was learned:**

- Rockets and flames are the same cliché in different costumes.
- A mark must be hardware or geometry, never weather. Curvy organic shapes read as stickers next to hard-edged type.
- Three bars alone read as a hamburger menu and, more importantly, felt like too little.
- The homepage mockup works with **no mark at all**, which is itself evidence that the wordmark can carry the masthead indefinitely.

**Standing constraint:** you can add a mark later and it reads as refinement. You cannot quietly remove one; that's a rebrand. Ship with the wordmark. Decide the mark when something is obviously right.
